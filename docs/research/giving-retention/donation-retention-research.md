# Donation and accounting record retention: research for the erasure design

Started 2026-10-11. Finished 2026-10-11. All sources read on 11 October 2026. Fact-gathering only, not legal advice.

## Summary

- **Periods found for the organisation's own records: 3 to 10 years.** Shortest: United States (3 years after the tax return is due or filed; 4 for payroll records). Longest: Italy (10 years from the last entry), Germany (up to 10 years) and the Philippines (10 years in practice). Most countries sit at 5, 6 or 7 years.
- **Donor-identifying records are required, in some form, in:** United Kingdom (Gift Aid declarations), Ireland (CHY3/CHY4 certificates), United States (name and address kept in the books, 501(c)(3) reports major donors), Canada (name and address on official receipts), New Zealand (name on receipt, address if held), South Africa (section 18A receipt), Germany (receipt copy), France (receipt shows full donor address), Spain (certificate has donor tax number), Italy (receipt shows donor details), Sweden, Norway and Denmark (gifts reported to the tax office with the donor's personal number), India (Forms 10BD and 10BE), Singapore (name and ID number for tax-deductible donations).
- **Australia is the exception in the list:** the receipt does not have to name the donor (it must name the fund and the ABN).
- **The period usually runs from the end of a year, not from the gift.** So the real keeping time after a gift is longer than the headline number.
- **Every EU or EEA country** can rely on GDPR Article 17(3)(b): erasure does not have to happen where the law requires the organisation to keep the data. The UK has the same wording. Countries outside Europe differ: some have no right to erase at all (Singapore, Canada, New Zealand), some have a "unless the law requires keeping it" clause (India, Kenya, Brazil, South Africa, Australia).
- **Reading quality.** Marked [R] = I read the legal text or official page myself. [S] = an official or authoritative page that I saw only through a search-tool summary, not in full. [2] = secondary source (law firm, news, vendor). Where nothing better exists, it says so.
- **NOT FOUND items:** donor-record rule for Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Jamaica, Brazil, Poland and the Philippines. Retention period for donor-specific records in Ireland, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Spain, Netherlands and Italy (only the general accounting period was found). Data-protection article for Ireland (national act), Norway, Denmark, Italy, Poland, Ghana (text), Jamaica, United States (no federal law), Philippines (right-to-erase text).
- **Not checked at all:** Scotland and Northern Ireland charity rules, Canadian provinces (including Quebec privacy law), US state laws, and any rule that is stricter for religious bodies.

## Notes

1. **How the periods count.** The UK, Canada, Germany, France, Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Singapore, Poland and Spain all start the clock at the END of a year or accounting period, not on the day of the gift. A gift in January is kept for the rest of that year plus the full period. A design that counts from the gift date will delete too early.
2. **Enduring or repeating gifts.** UK enduring Gift Aid declarations are kept while gifts continue, and the clock runs from the last gift [R]. Irish CHY3 certificates last for the signing year plus four more tax years [S]. Singapore and Dutch periodic gifts work similarly. A rule of "last gift plus period" fits all of these.
3. **Longer if there is an enquiry or audit.** UK (HMRC enquiries), South Africa (audit or no return filed), Ireland (investigation or claim begun) and Kenya (disputes, per law-firm commentary [2]) all extend the period until the matter ends. The design needs a "hold" flag.
4. **Which records the law actually names.** Several countries name the document (UK declaration, German receipt copy, Irish certificate, Singapore donation record, Spanish certificate), not "all donor data". Contact details used only for newsletters or events are not covered by any of these rules. That supports keeping only the receipted-gift records and erasing the rest.
5. **Restrict, then delete.** Germany (BDSG § 35(3)), Spain (blocking), South Africa (POPIA 14(6)) and Kenya (s.40(3)) expect the data to be locked away, not deleted, until the period ends. Spain's regulator adds that blocking is not itself a reason to keep data, so the legal duty must be named. The UK regulator also says to record which law requires the keeping.
6. **No right to erase.** Singapore (PDPA), Canada (PIPEDA, none found) and New Zealand (none found) give no general right to ask for deletion. Erasure requests there are best handled by the retention-limit rules.
7. **Conflicts and doubts, stated plainly.**
   - **India:** 6 years (old law) against 7 years (draft 2026 rules). The 2025 Income-tax Act replaced the 1961 Act; the final notified rules were not located.
   - **Poland:** one source says approved annual accounts are kept at least 5 years, another says permanently.
   - **Germany:** the donation-receipt copy has no stated period in § 50 EStDV. I treated it as an accounting voucher (8 years). A tax adviser should confirm; older guidance used 10.
   - **France:** the search summary said L102 B has a paragraph for gift-receiving bodies. The text I read had no such paragraph. I did not find a donor-receipt retention rule.
   - **Jamaica:** the Data Protection Act's start date is reported as 1 December 2021 by one source and as not yet in force by another.
   - **Norway:** the retention section is being amended from 1 January 2027.
   - **Australia:** the penalty for poor DGR records is given as 20 penalty units on one ATO page and 10 on another. Not relevant to the period.
8. **Wrong guesses I started with (corrected).** I began with a 10-year assumption for Germany's receipt copy, a 6-year assumption for Ireland's charity records, a 7-year assumption for Jamaica, and "Rule 11AA" for India. None was supported. The report uses only what was found.
9. **Recalled from memory, NOT verified.** France: a 10-year commercial-code accounting period (Code de commerce L123-22). Norway: EEA application of GDPR Art 17. California: a "legal obligation" exception to deletion. Use as leads only.
10. **Source access problems.** GhaLII (Ghana) and the Philippines privacy commission site blocked automated reading. The Kenya Data Protection Act was read from Kenya Law instead of the ODPC PDF. Several PDFs (IRS 4221-PC, Nigerian Act, Irish manual) were read after converting them locally.
11. **Scope not covered:** Scotland and Northern Ireland charity law, Canadian provinces including Quebec, US states, religious-body exemptions, and any rule specific to schools or government-funded bodies.
