# Rendered pins for tools/erasure-pages-selftest.php (#703; the owner's decisions D-016, D-110 to D-113).
#
# Each pin is what a member or an administrator RECEIVES for one part of a page that tells an erased
# person what stays: My data's rows for their account and for the four kinds of worker record, the
# privacy notice's whole "Retention policy" section (drawn in all four states of its giving sentence),
# the help page's whole section on what an erasure keeps, and Delete my account's "What is kept" section
# (drawn under a hold at the organisation and under one on the whole installation). Since #940, also the
# privacy PAGE's whole "Retention" section in each of section L's seven activity-log states (D-117, D-118),
# step 5 of the administrators' first steps, and the Retention page's activity-log card, subtitle and the
# card's explanation in both views. Since #927, also the expense-claim row on Request erasure and on My
# data, whose label must say the claim keeps the account number. A section runs from its heading to the
# next heading of the SAME or a HIGHER level (an <h3> section ends at the next <h3>, <h2> or <h1>).
#
# WHAT IS COMPARED, exactly, with every run of white space made one space and nothing else changed:
#   ANCESTORS: the tag and attributes of every element around the region, up to and including <body>
#   REGION:    the region's own markup, attributes included
# The one value allowed to differ is <body data-print-date>, today's date, written "(today)".
# The region must be found EXACTLY ONCE on the page, or its pin fails.
#
# WHAT IS NOT COMPARED, said plainly: a <style> rule or a script elsewhere on the page that hides or
# rewrites the region. Whether text is visible is a question only a real browser can answer, and none
# runs in this test.
#
# FORM: a line starting "#" is a note; "== <name>" starts a pin and the next line is its value. The
# test fails if a name is given twice, a line is none of these, or a pin is compared by no check.
#
# TO RE-PIN, on purpose: run the test with SELFTEST_WRITE_RENDERED_PINS=<file>, read what it wrote,
# then copy its pins in here below these notes.

# #927: an expense claim keeps the claimant's account number (D-113), and its LABEL says so, on both pages
# that print one. My data's badge printed the instruction's own word ("anonymise", "retain") until #927; it
# prints the same words as Request erasure now, which is why the five My data pins below changed too.
# Since #927's check round 1 My data's badge says what WOULD happen ("would be ... if you ask to be
# erased"): the page lists what is held NOW, and bare words ("deleted") were untrue about the present.

== help kept list
ANCESTORS: <body data-portal-name="Portal" data-print-date="(today)" data-portal-authenticated="1"> <main class="portal-main" id="main-content" role="main"> <div class="container"> <div class="portal-card p-4 mb-4" id="giving-records"> REGION: <h3 class="h6 mt-3">What an erasure keeps, and what it does not reach</h3><p> This was written out in full on 2 November 2026, by going through every table in the database rather than relying on memory. Three kinds of thing survive an erasure, and they are not the same kind of thing at all. </p><ul> <li> <strong>Kept because the law or the organisation's duty requires it.</strong> Giving records as above. <strong>Expense claims, which HMRC requires for six years — and the claimant is kept with them.</strong> That was decided deliberately on 3 November 2026: a record of a payment that cannot say who was paid is of little use as one. This page used to say the claimant's name came off the claim, and that was wrong in the reassuring direction — the erasure does try to remove the account link, and the database will not allow that column to be empty, so the report of the erasure records that it could do nothing there. The account itself is emptied, so the claim points at an account with no name on it, but the account number stays — and <strong>that does not always stop the claim naming the claimant</strong>: if they made pastoral visits, opened care cases, recorded DBS checks or checked children in or out, those records keep their name beside the same number (see below), so anyone who can read the database or a backup can match the claim back to them. The receipts attached to a claim are kept with it; since 3 November 2026 their names are made up by the portal, so they carry nothing typed by whoever uploaded them (a receipt uploaded before then may still carry its original file name on the server). The adults authorised to collect a child, because the organisation's duty of care to that child does not end because an adult asked to be erased. A Gift Aid declaration, with the network address it was made from, because that is part of the evidence it was made. <br> <strong>Who made a pastoral visit, opened a care case, recorded a DBS (safeguarding) check, or checked a child in or out.</strong> When a member of staff or a volunteer asks to be erased, those records keep their <strong>name and account number</strong> as the person who did it — including a visit they made on a case about themselves. That was decided deliberately (3 and 10 November 2026): a safeguarding record that cannot say who carried it out is worse for the people it exists to protect. Their account itself is still emptied — contact details, sign-in, everything else — and their name and account number are the only things about them left on those records, unless the record is also about them (a visit on a case about them, or their own DBS check). <strong>Be clear about what that costs:</strong> because the number stays beside the name, anyone who can read the database or a backup can use those records to work out which of the erased person's other kept records were theirs — an expense claim, for instance, which also keeps the number. Somebody asking to be erased cannot expect their name to come off these records. The name is the one their account had when the record was made. Two limits, said plainly: a worker erased before this change cannot be given their name back, so their older pastoral visits still show “Somebody no longer recorded” on the case page (no page shows who opened a case, who recorded a DBS check or who checked a child in or out); and what a visit says about the person <em>being cared for</em> is still emptied when <em>they</em> ask to be erased. </li> <li> <strong>Kept because it is the organisation's own record, not the person's.</strong> A note about a piece of equipment, the condition it was in, who serviced it. A group's description and its meeting notes. A venue's address. What was changed in a settings page. These stay and the account link is cut. <strong>The change history is a special case and was improved on 2 November 2026:</strong> an entry about the person's own account or their memberships now has its before-and-after values emptied too, because a change to an account recorded the row as it was — in practice their name and e-mail address. Who made each change, when, and which record it was are kept, so the history still shows the account was changed — and that means the entry still holds the account's number. <strong>The change history is kept for ever</strong> (decided on 10 November 2026), and the stored copy of some <em>other</em> record — an expense claim, a loan — can hold the person's account number too. <br> It now covers an entry about their account and about four kinds of membership — which organisations, departments, user groups and roles they belonged to. The last three were added on 2 November 2026; before that they were not reached. Those entries record account numbers and no names, which does not make them anonymous: the number can be matched back to the person through any record that keeps their name beside it. They are covered so that the day one of them starts recording a name, it is already dealt with. <br> One limit, so that nothing here is read as more than it is. An entry about some <em>other</em> record whose text happens to mention them is <strong>not</strong> reached, because nothing on such a row says they are in it — and searching the text for a name would match somebody else of the same name and miss a nickname. </li> <li> <strong>Kept because emptying it would take something from somebody else.</strong> Two of these were decided deliberately on 1 November 2026, and they are not oversights. The address a small group meets at is very often a member's <strong>home</strong> — but nothing records whose, so emptying it would blank the meeting place of groups that have nothing to do with the person asking. A name typed in for a visitor with no account stays on the attendance register, because there is no account to match it to, which is the only reason it was typed in the first place. </li> <li> <strong>Emptied on request now, where it is the person's own words about themselves.</strong> A <strong>prayer request's subject and body</strong> and a <strong>prayer partner's note about them</strong> are emptied, and so is a <strong>pastoral case's summary</strong> — which is somebody else's description of the person's circumstances, and sat beside visit notes that were already emptied. The rows stay, so the request still shows that one was made and answered, and the case still shows who opened it and when. </li> <li> <strong>Cleared on a timer rather than on request, because nothing on the row says who it is about.</strong> Each of these is kept for a period you set, and then emptied or deleted by the nightly job. An erasure request cannot reach them, because there is no account number on the row to match — and a rule that searched the text for a name would match somebody else of the same name and miss a nickname. <ul> <li> <strong>Messages from a payment provider.</strong> A payment message routinely carries the payer's name, e-mail address and billing address. The body is now kept only once the message has been proved genuine, and emptied after the period you set. <strong>These used to be kept whole and for ever.</strong> </li> <li> <strong>Messages the portal sent to another system</strong> — a registration carries somebody's full name, an event sent in by a visitor carries their e-mail address. Emptied after the same period, but <strong>only once the message has been delivered or given up on</strong>: one still waiting to be sent keeps its body, because that is what a retry sends. </li> <li> <strong>A recording's written-out transcript</strong>, and a second searchable copy of it — whatever was said aloud. Deleted after the period you set. </li> <li> <strong>The fault log's detail and the address asked for</strong>, which can quote a name or an e-mail address. These expire on the fault log's own timer. </li> </ul> <strong>If the period matters for somebody who has asked to be erased, shorten it, or deal with those rows directly and record that you have.</strong> </li> <li> <strong>The activity log: what each record says.</strong> On the person's own records an erasure removes the account number, the network address, the browser and the session details, but what each record says is kept as it was written — and some records, the person's own and other people's, give a full name, an e-mail address or an account number. Those words go only when the record does: the nightly job, once it has been set up, deletes it after the period set for the organisation it was made under, which that organisation's administrator sets within the installation's, on Admin, Maintenance, Retention (a record that belongs to no organisation follows the installation's period). <strong>Where the installation has chosen to keep the activity log for ever, nothing deletes them in an organisation that has not chosen a number of days of its own, nor in records that belong to no organisation</strong>; an organisation that has chosen a number of days is still cleared after it. The installation's “for ever” is the longest an organisation may keep the log, not a rule for all. </li> <li> <strong>Kept on purpose, because the words are about the organisation rather than the person.</strong> Decided on 3 November 2026: a <strong>presenter's name typed in by hand</strong> on a service plan or a recording, a licence seat's <strong>device name and label</strong>, and a small group's <strong>meeting notes</strong>. These describe how the organisation runs, and emptying them would take a record from the organisation to no benefit of the person asking. </li> <li> <strong>Emptied on request since 3 November 2026, because the person is the one the record is ABOUT.</strong> Two of these were decided that day, and both are the same decision: the portal had been treating the person as <em>somebody who acted on a record</em> when they were in fact its subject, and emptying text on an actor's name would wipe somebody else's details, so the portal would not do it. <ul> <li> <strong>Why somebody wanted a room, and the note refusing them.</strong> A booking is made by the person it is about, so their stated reason is emptied. The booking itself stays, so the room's history is intact, and a booking somebody else made and this person only <em>approved</em> keeps its reason. (This page used to list this under "not reached"; that stopped being true when the change was made and the page had not been brought into line.) </li> <li> <strong>The contact details typed on to an equipment loan.</strong> The loan form asks for a telephone number or e-mail address for whoever borrowed the item, whether they are a member or not, so on a member's own loan that is their own number. It is emptied now. The loan stays — which item went out, when it went, when it came back — and a loan somebody else borrowed, which this person only approved or asked for, keeps that borrower's details. </li> </ul> </li> <li> <strong>Not reached, which is a fault rather than a decision.</strong> One is left, with a number against it so it is not forgotten: <strong>a salvation card's prayer request</strong>. Nothing on the card says whose it is — the only account on it is the follow-up worker it was given to, and emptying on that would wipe a card about somebody else because a volunteer left. <strong>If somebody asks to be erased and this matters to them, deal with it directly and record that you have.</strong> </li> <li> <strong>Deleted, not merely unnamed — and since 3 November 2026 the portal asks whose record it is.</strong> An <strong>event registration</strong> holds the registered person's name, date of birth, allergies and medical notes, so when it is theirs the whole row is deleted. The portal used to find those by who <em>typed the form in</em>, with nothing to say whether that was the same person — so erasing a volunteer who had signed somebody else's child up <strong>deleted that child's registration</strong>, and that child's family had asked for nothing. The registration form now asks a signed-in person whether it is their own, and an erasure deletes only the ones that are; for anyone else it removes their link and leaves the registration alone. <br> Two things to know. For registrations made <em>before</em> that change, whoever typed one in is treated as the person it is for — true of anyone who registered themselves, which is most of them, and where a volunteer typed one for a child the child's name is still on it, so nothing was lost. And a registration made by somebody with no account at all still cannot be found by an erasure request; those are deleted on the event's own time limit instead. </li> </ul><p> Everything above is written down, table by table and column by column, with the reason for each, in the portal's own list of where personal information lives. An automatic check refuses to let a new place holding somebody's contact details be added without a decision being written for it — but <strong>that check cannot judge free text</strong>, so a note somebody types a name into will not be caught by it. </p>
